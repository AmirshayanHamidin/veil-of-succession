# Variant A — Literary Thriller
*A work of political fiction. All persons, offices, and organizations are fictitious.*
*Register: image-rich, interior, symbolic. The prose knows it is prose.*

---

## Opening

A qanat is an old kind of mercy: a channel dug underground, by hand, across
miles of desert, so that water can move beneath the heat that would steal it.
The surface never sees the water. The surface only sees the gardens.

In a Tehran-inspired capital, in a near future that feels like a held breath,
the Supreme Guide is dying. Ayatollah Mahdavi has been dying for a year, the
way institutions die — attended, denied, and photographed. When he goes, the
republic's future passes through a single room: the Council of Custodians,
twelve old men who will name what comes next. Every faction in the country is
watching that room. Only one man has already furnished it.

General Farrokh Azadi commands the intelligence directorate of the Guardian
Corps, and he has learned the lesson of every strongman before him: you do not
seize a throne, you seize the hour in which the throne is empty. He is
building that hour by hand — a staged foreign sabotage, a manufactured
emergency, a pliable cleric to elevate, and a dossier of blackmail on all
twelve Custodians, kept where he can reach it and they cannot: the palace
where the Council meets. He does not want to be seen ruling. He wants to rule
from behind the veil, which is where all real power in this country has ever
lived.

Against him, the water underground: a network of officers still loyal to the
constitution, who call themselves The Qanat. And the network's finest
instrument is a twenty-nine-year-old woman with a famous last name.

## Core canon, in this register

**Yasaman "Yas" Rahimi** is the daughter of Custodian Hossein Rahimi, one of
the twelve, one of the moderates — which is to say, one of the blackmailed.
She was educated abroad and came home fluent in two kinds of language: the
signals work and infiltration craft The Qanat trained into her, and the older
dialect of privilege. Her father's name opens gates her tradecraft never
could. The Rahimi girl is waved through; nobody asks the Rahimi girl anything.
She is elegant because elegance is camouflage, controlled because control is
survival, and dry-humored because the humor is the last room in the house that
is entirely hers. The arithmetic she lives inside is simple and unforgivable:
every gate her name opens is a gate her father stands behind. If she is
caught, he hangs with her.

**Hossein Rahimi** suspects the plot and cannot prove it and will not flee,
because a man who has spent his life inside an institution believes, past all
evidence, that the institution can be saved from inside. His daughter loves
him for this and cannot forgive him for it, and has never told him what she
is.

**Azadi**, fifty-eight, war hero, is the most dangerous kind of villain: a
sincere one. He believes the republic survives only through a strong hand and
has the scars to argue it. He is soft-spoken. The calmer he is, the worse it
is.

**Darya** is the voice in Yas's ear — handler, compass, almost-mother, warmth
folded inside dryness like a blade in cloth.

**The tone** is restraint over spectacle. The palace is beautiful and the rot
is quiet; violence is a failure of craft, not a pleasure; the roses are real
and so are the cameras. And the ending honors the qanat it is named for: the
water moves, the garden survives another season, and no one on the surface
ever knows. The succession is safe — for now. What it costs her is the story.
