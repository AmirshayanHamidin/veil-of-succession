# Variant C — TLOU Intimate
*A work of political fiction. All persons, offices, and organizations are fictitious.*
*Register: close, sensory, character-first. Politics is the weather; the story is the people in it.*

---

## Opening

Yasaman Rahimi is twenty-nine, and the last time her father hugged her, he
smelled of rosewater and old paper, and she stood very still so she wouldn't
have to decide what to do with her hands. That was three weeks ago, at his
door. He asked if she was eating. She said yes, Baba. He looked at her a
second too long — the way he looks at documents that don't quite add up — and
then let it go, because he wanted to.

He is one of the twelve. Custodian Hossein Rahimi, a moderate on the Council
that will choose what this country becomes when the old man at the top
finally dies — and the old man is dying now, truly, whatever the bulletins
say. Her father goes to the secret sessions in the palace and comes home
smaller each time. He knows something is wrong inside that room. He can't
prove it. He won't run. When she begged him once — carefully, hypothetically,
a daughter worrying out loud — he smiled and said a man doesn't leave his
post because the weather turns.

What he doesn't know, what he can never know, is that his daughter went away
to study and came back recruited. The Qanat found her abroad: officers loyal
to the constitution, watching General Azadi build his quiet coup — the staged
sabotage, the manufactured emergency, the blackmail dossier on all twelve
Custodians, her father's file in it somewhere, its contents a thing she
makes herself not imagine. They trained her in locks and signals and the art
of being unremarkable. Then they told her the truth she already knew: the
Rahimi name walks through palace gates that no forged paper ever will.

So she goes over the wall with her father's name as her armor, and the armor
is the trap. Every step she takes inside that palace, she is spending him.
If she is caught, they will not just hang her. They will hang the man who
taught her to ride a bicycle in a courtyard full of roses, and they will
make him watch her go first.

She is elegant, because her mother was. She is controlled, because the
alternative is not available. She is funny at the worst possible moments,
because if she stops being funny she will start being afraid, and there is a
voice in her ear — Darya, dry and steady, warmth hidden in it like sugar at
the bottom of the cup — who always hears the difference.

## Core canon, in this register

**Yas** wants one impossible thing: for her father to survive without ever
learning what she did to save him. The mission is the plot; that wish is the
story.

**Hossein** loves his daughter and underestimates her, which in this family
is the same gesture. His faith is in institutions; hers is in him.

**General Azadi**, 58, is not a monster to himself. He buried friends for
this republic and believes only a strong hand keeps it standing — his. He
speaks softly to Yas when he finally finds her, almost kindly. That's the
frightening part.

**The tone** stays close to the body: cold stone through gloves, the weight
of a stolen keycard, her father's voice through a listening-post wall. Sneak,
don't slaughter — every guard is somebody's Omid Shirazi, sneaking a
cigarette he promised his wife he'd quit. And the ending is won and unhealed
at once. The succession is safe, for now. She saved her father. She can
never tell him.
